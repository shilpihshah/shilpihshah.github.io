AI-native vs. AI-washed: how I tell them apart

Almost every software pitch I see now has "AI" in its first sentence. That's no longer a signal on its own. Here's the short list of questions I use to tell which companies are actually built around AI and which ones just added it to the slide.

The incentive to relabel is obvious. Carta found that in Q1 2026, 83% of SaaS venture capital went to AI startups. When the money moves that sharply, the pitch decks move with it. And the money is real. Menlo Ventures estimates enterprises spent $37 billion on generative AI in 2025, up from $11.5 billion in 2024, and $19 billion of that went to the application layer. So the question isn't whether AI applications are a real market. It's which companies will actually capture that spend.

Four years of tech M&A advisory at PwC taught me one habit I still rely on: take the narrative out and see what's left. On a sell-side deal, "adjusted EBITDA" gets the same treatment as "AI-powered" does today. You rebuild it from the ground up. These are the questions I ask now.

1. Does the product do the work, or help someone do it?

An AI-washed product adds a copilot next to an existing seat: a summary button, a drafting assistant. An AI-native product owns a whole unit of work from start to finish, like resolving a ticket, reconciling an invoice, or producing a first-pass diligence memo. The test I use: if you took the model out, would the product still make sense as ordinary SaaS? If it would, the AI is a feature and not the foundation.

2. What are they actually charging for?

Pricing shows what a company really believes about itself. Seat-based pricing assumes a person is still doing the work. When the software does the work, pricing tends to follow outcomes. Intercom is the clearest public example: its Fin agent is priced at $0.99 per outcome, and it's only billed when the conversation actually gets resolved. I don't require outcome pricing at Series A. I do want founders who've thought about it, because if their product works, it reduces the number of seats their customers need.

3. Does the data compound, or does it just sit there?

"We have proprietary data" is the new "we have a network effect." What I look for is a loop: every task the product completes makes the next one better, cheaper, or harder to copy. Corrections from users, edge cases, approvals, and outcomes all get captured in a form the next model run can learn from. A static dataset loses value as models get better. A feedback loop gains value.

4. Does the next model release help or hurt?

This is my favorite question because founders answer it so differently. For a thin wrapper, a better frontier model is a threat, because the gap between the startup and a raw API call gets smaller. For an AI-native company, a better model is a tailwind: margins go up, tasks it can handle get harder, and the parts that are hard to build (integrations, permissions, evaluation, the customer relationship) are still there.

5. Can they back up what they claim?

Regulators are now asking this too. In 2024 the SEC settled with two investment advisers for "AI washing," meaning they marketed AI capabilities they didn't have. In August 2026 it brought a settled action against an AI startup, GenesisAI, over alleged misstatements about revenue, valuation, and a customer waitlist the SEC said didn't exist. Those are extreme cases. The everyday version is subtler: a demo that is mostly hand-built, or "agents" that are really a team of contractors. I ask to see their evaluation results and failure logs, not just the demo.

Where I could be wrong

Being AI-washed isn't fatal. Some incumbents with real distribution will add AI well and win anyway, and some "wrappers" will turn into systems of record because they got the customer relationship first. My framework favors companies built from the start for a world where software does the work, and that's a bet, not a law. What I'm sure of is that in a market where everyone says "AI," the companies that win will be the ones whose products would stop making sense without it.
