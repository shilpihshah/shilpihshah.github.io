AI-native vs. AI-washed: how I tell them apart

Almost every software pitch I see now has "AI" in its first sentence. That's no longer a signal on its own. Here's the short list of questions I try to find answers to when I look at an AI company, so I can tell which ones are actually built around AI and which ones just added it to the slide.

The incentive to relabel is obvious. According to Carta, "over 60 cents of each dollar of venture capital investment on Carta in Q1 2026 went to AI companies, the highest share recorded to date." A big part of that went to foundational model companies: the labs that build the large, general-purpose AI models other products run on, like Anthropic, OpenAI, and xAI (now part of SpaceX). Carta found that those companies alone accounted for "14.2% of total capital and nearly a quarter of AI capital." Menlo Ventures estimates enterprises spent $37 billion on generative AI in 2025, up from $11.5 billion in 2024, and $19 billion of that went to the application layer. Looking ahead, Gartner forecasts that worldwide spending on AI software will grow from about $462 billion in 2026 to about $656 billion in 2027. Gartner also notes that much of this comes from existing software vendors "rapidly embedding agentic AI within their existing products," which is part of why telling the difference matters. So the question isn't whether AI applications are a real market. It's which companies will actually capture that spend.

I spent four years in tech M&A advisory at PwC, and the most useful habit I took from it is simple: don't start from the company's story, start from the evidence. In diligence, that meant checking claims against the underlying data, like contracts, usage, and what customers actually pay for. I try to do the same with AI claims. These are the questions I try to answer.

1. Does the product do the work, or help someone do it?

An AI-washed product adds a copilot next to an existing seat: a summary button, a drafting assistant. An AI-native product owns a whole unit of work from start to finish, like resolving a ticket, reconciling an invoice, or producing a first-pass diligence memo. The test I use: if you took the model out, would the product still make sense as ordinary SaaS? If it would, the AI is a feature and not the foundation.

I saw a version of this from the inside at PwC. When the firm rolled out ChatPwC, its internal AI assistant, it felt more like a searchbot than anything else: a chat window that sat next to our work instead of doing any of it. I personally barely used it. What PwC is rolling out now looks different. In 2026 it started bringing Anthropic's Claude Cowork to its U.S. teams, and in the deals practice the stated goal is to rework diligence, value creation, and integration with "agents working alongside deal teams." The first was a tool you had to remember to open. The second is starting to take on pieces of the work itself. To me, that's a preview of what AI-native looks like in the future of work.

2. What are they actually charging for?

Pricing shows what a company really believes about itself. Most software is sold by the seat: the customer pays for each person who uses it. That works when the software helps a person do their job. But if the AI actually does the work, the customer needs fewer people doing it, and so fewer seats. A company selling seats could end up shrinking its own revenue as its product gets better. That's why outcome-based pricing can be a sign of an AI-native company: the customer pays for the work that got done, not for who logged in.

In reality, though, most AI products are still priced per seat, and that isn't automatically a red flag. Seat pricing makes sense when the AI helps a person do their job better but gets riskier when the AI replaces the work, because the better the product works, the fewer seats the customer needs. The companies that have thought through their pricing strategy between outcome vs. seat-based are already ahead of the game.

My take: I think the move to outcome-based pricing might be compelling for B2B sales. Consumers understand a monthly subscription. It's simple and predictable, and nobody wants to wonder what one question to an app is going to cost them. Businesses already track things like tickets closed and invoices processed, so paying per outcome fits how they budget. For consumer AI products, I'd expect subscriptions to stay the default, even when the product is genuinely AI-native.

3. Does the data compound, or does it just sit there?

"We have proprietary data" is the new "we have a network effect." I do think owning the right data can make a company more compelling. But having data isn't the same as having data that gets more valuable over time. What I look for is a loop: every task the product completes makes the next one better.

Abridge, the AI medical scribe, is a good recent example of the first part. Because it sits in the exam room, it has de-identified clinical conversations that a general-purpose model never sees. In June 2026 it announced it is working with Nvidia to build a foundation model for clinical conversations, trained on that de-identified data and built for its own platform. Shopify shows what the loop looks like once it's running. In August 2026 it described how conversations where its Sidekick assistant falls short get turned into training data every day. For one of its agents, the smaller model trained that way now beats frontier-model quality at an estimated 96% lower serving cost. A competitor can license the same frontier model. It can't license years of real conversations and corrections. A static dataset loses value as models get better. A feedback loop like that gains value.

4. Does the next model release help or hurt?

This is my favorite question because the answers vary so much. Every few months, one of the big AI labs releases a better model. The question is whether that makes the startup more valuable or less. For a thin wrapper, meaning a product that is mostly a nice interface on top of someone else's model, a better model is a threat. The better the underlying model gets, the less a customer needs the wrapper, because they can get similar results from ChatGPT or Claude directly. Early AI writing tools felt this first. Jasper, one of the best-known, cut roles in July 2023 and refocused on marketing teams at mid-size and enterprise companies, after what its CEO described as "an explosion of consumer AI tools." For an AI-native company, a better model is a tailwind: margins go up, the tasks it can handle get harder, and the parts that are hard to build (integrations, permissions, evaluation, the customer relationship) are still there.

5. Can they back up what they claim?

Regulators are now asking this too. In 2024 the SEC settled with two investment advisers for "AI washing," meaning they marketed AI capabilities they didn't have. In August 2026 it brought a settled action against an AI startup, GenesisAI, over alleged misstatements about revenue, valuation, and a customer waitlist the SEC said didn't exist. Those are extreme cases. I don't think the more common version today is faking AI. It's a product that is only AI: a prompt on top of a frontier model, shipped quickly, with little thought about whether it's a viable business. Being made with AI, or vibe-coded, doesn't make something AI-native. I'd want to see real thought about viability: depth in the customer's workflow, a reason customers stay, and evidence it works beyond the demo, like evaluation results and how it handles failures. With so much AI out there now, I think tangible touchpoints count for more than they used to: customer testimonials, references I can actually call, and repeat usage. Those are a lot harder to fake.

Where I could be wrong

Being AI-washed isn't fatal. Some incumbents with real distribution will add AI well and win anyway, and some "wrappers" will turn into systems of record because they got the customer relationship first. My framework favors companies built from the start for a world where software does the work, and that's a bet, not a law. What I'm sure of is that in a market where everyone says "AI," the companies that win will be the ones whose products would stop making sense without it.
