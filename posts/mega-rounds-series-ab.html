27 companies took a third of the money. What that means at Series A and B.

The Q3 venture numbers are out, and the headline is concentration. If you invest at Series A and B, I think the right reaction is to stop using the headline averages and price off what the median company is actually raising.

First, the numbers. Crunchbase counted $159 billion of global venture funding in Q3 2026, and a record 27 companies raised rounds of $1 billion or more, up from 16 in Q2 and 14 in Q1. Those 27 companies took roughly a third of all the capital. AI startups took $102 billion of it, or 64%. In the U.S., the PitchBook-NVCA Venture Monitor puts deal value through September at $515.8 billion, with AI at a record 82.7% of it.

Those totals overstate how much money is actually available to most companies. According to the same report, deals of $100 million or more were about 5% of Q3 deal count but 75% of deal value. AI's share of quarterly deal value fell from 88.4% in Q1 to 65.9% in Q3 as frontier-lab rounds tapered off, while its share of deal count held steady at around 45%. So the number of AI companies getting funded is stable. Only the dollar totals are swinging.

The medians tell a calmer story

Carta's Q2 2026 benchmarks put the median U.S. Series A at $14.7 million on a $76.3 million post-money valuation, and the median Series B at $23.3 million on $166.2 million. That's large, but not wild. The gap shows up in specific categories: in Q1, Carta saw foundation-model startups raising Series A rounds at around a $300 million median valuation, versus roughly $55 million for non-AI companies. Those really are two separate markets. Applying one market's pricing to the other is how investors end up overpaying, or skipping good companies because they look expensive.

What I take from it as an early-stage investor

  1. The opportunity is in the middle. Mega-rounds mostly go to compute-heavy businesses: frontier labs, data centers, chips. Application companies that build on those models and don't need a billion dollars of GPUs can still be priced like normal Series A companies. That's where I'd want to spend my time.
  2. Getting the ownership you need will be harder than getting the deal. Crunchbase reports that rounds of $100 million or more made up about half of Q3 early-stage funding. When multistage funds preempt at the A, a disciplined Series A/B fund has to win on something other than check size: helping with go-to-market, recruiting, and being there for the first enterprise sale.
  3. Underwrite the exit you can actually see. This is where my M&A background matters most. PitchBook counts 992 active unicorns worth $5.7 trillion, and only 18 IPOs in Q3. Meanwhile strategic buyers are active: Crunchbase notes Nvidia's $12.9 billion bid for Hugging Face and Stripe's $7.5 billion deal for OpenRouter. If the most likely exit is a strategic sale, the entry price has to make sense in a strategic sale too.

What would change my mind

If IPOs reopen in a real way, late-stage valuations get validated and the mega-round strategy looks smart in hindsight. And concentration isn't automatically a bubble. Some of these companies really are growing faster than anything software has seen. But for investors at my stage, I think the discipline is clear: benchmark against companies like the one in front of you, not against the headlines.
